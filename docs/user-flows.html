01

The role map

Five working roles and the documents that pass between them. Read the arrows as responsibility changing hands — not as data moving.

Three properties of this map are worth stating plainly, because they are the ones that will be argued about.

  • Nothing enters except through the central store. No outlet takes a delivery directly from a vendor. It costs a little convenience and buys a single, trustworthy point of entry where batch, expiry and landed cost are captured once.
  • The counter never talks to procurement. A shortage at a counter becomes a request to the store; only the store's own shortfall becomes a purchase requisition. That keeps buying decisions in one place and stops three outlets independently ordering milk.
  • Nobody approves a purchase order but the buyer who raised it. PO_APPROVAL_LIMIT stamps a badge on an order over the slab; nothing refuses it and no second role stands behind the number — see §08, below, and docs/ua-spec.html §04.

03

The five roles

For each one: what they see the moment they sign in, the screens they move through, the loop they repeat all day, and — just as important — what the system will not let them do. These are RoleSchema's own five (packages/contract/src/schemas/common.ts), not a proposal.

CO

Counter Operator

2 seeded users · Coffee Shop, Snack Kiosk

Signs in and lands on Point of Sale
Point of Sale→Bills→ Stock in Hand→Stock Requests→Pick Tickets

Signs in with an employee number and password, checked against the server — no shift, no terminal binding, no PIN. Their day is one loop: take an order, tap or scan, tender, print. Everything else is interruption — an item goes dark, they raise a request, and later they collect a ticket.

Two things only Phase 6 gave them: a support ticket of their own, and the door to withdraw a shop-to-shop transfer they raised themselves, from the outbound card on their own Tickets screen.

Decisions they own

  • What goes on the bill and how it is tendered
  • Whether to switch an item off at their own counter
  • Raising a stock request, and asking a peer shop directly
  • Withdrawing a shop-to-shop transfer this outlet raised, before anyone collects it

Hard stops. Cannot change a price, approve a request, issue a ticket, or open any purchasing screen — the modules are absent from their sidebar, not merely disabled.

OM

Outlet Manager

1 seeded user · all three outlets

Signs in and lands on Approvals
Approvals→ Items & Stock→Price Lists→Product On/Off

The one role that stands over every counter rather than working one. Their whole day is deciding what other people asked for: approving, trimming or rejecting a stock request — never past what the central store can still promise once every open ticket and other approval is netted off.

Their distinctive power is price: both lists, every outlet's menu, and the one thing no role can override — a traded item's printed MRP.

Decisions they own

  • Approving, trimming or rejecting a stock request
  • Selling price on both lists, at every outlet
  • Adding or removing a menu item, at every outlet
  • Switching an item on or off at any outlet, not only their own

Hard stops. Cannot receive from a vendor, raise a purchase order, or price a traded item above its printed MRP — that ceiling has no override, for anyone.

SK

Store Keeper

1 seeded user · Central Store

Signs in and lands on Issue Desk
Issue Desk→Stock in Hand→ Requisitions→Rate Contracts→Reports

The busiest role and the hub of the map. Approved requests arrive all day and become tickets; deliveries land against purchase orders and become goods receipts, split between the central store's shelf and quarantine. Stock leaves only at handover, on the collector's own six-digit code.

They land on the issue desk rather than a dashboard because a ticket not yet issued is a counter that cannot restock. Two new things Phase 6 gave them: the stock ledger and a payer's credit, read straight off the server rather than guessed at.

Decisions they own

  • Issuing a ticket against an approved request, and handing it over — on the code, or the labelled override
  • Raising a requisition to procurement; maintaining rate contracts
  • Adding a product directly to the master
  • Answering a shop's request for something not on the master

Hard stops. Cannot change a purchase rate, sell anything, or approve their own stock movement. Receiving into a store they are not assigned to is not offered at all — there is only one central store, and it is theirs.

KI

Kitchen In-charge

1 seeded user · Central Kitchen

Signs in and lands on Kitchen order board
Orders→Make & Distribute→ Kitchen Stock→Stock Requests→Pick Tickets

Walks the board one press at a time — New, Accepted, In kitchen, Ready — and dispatches from any of those stages the moment an order is genuinely ready, whatever the board still shows. Closing a batch is the single most consequential action in the system: raw materials leave stock and finished goods appear, measured against what is free to promise, not merely what is on the shelf.

A short cover refuses the whole batch, naming the ingredient; nothing moves.

Decisions they own

  • Whether the kitchen holds enough to make a batch, and how large
  • Dispatching a production order and distributing stock — both all-or-nothing
  • Pushing finished goods out to the outlets
  • Handing a ticket over from the kitchen, on the collector's code or the labelled override

Hard stops. Cannot set the selling price of what they produce, and cannot close a batch that would take an ingredient's cover negative.

PO

Procurement Officer

1 seeded user · not tied to one outlet

Signs in and lands on Requisitions
Requisitions→Procurement List→ Purchase Orders→Vendors→New Products

Never types a shopping list from nothing. The procurement list is a live query over every requisition line approved but not yet ordered — not a table anyone maintains by hand. Their work is sourcing: a rate contract's price when one covers the item, the item's own standard cost when none does.

Decisions they own

  • Approving, trimming or declining a requisition, whole or line by line
  • Which vendor, and at what rate, when no contract covers it
  • Receiving a delivery — the 2% tolerance, and quarantine for anything rejected
  • Sourcing a new product a shop has asked for

Hard stops. Cannot raise a requisition against their own decision, approve their own purchase order at any value — there is no second approver to hand it to, only a badge on their own list — and cannot receive without a delivery to check against.

Journey: a counter runs dry

The loop that runs dozens of times a day and touches four roles. If only one flow in this document gets tested properly, make it this one.

06

Journey: one counter supplies another

Four in the afternoon, the store is shut, and the restaurant two floors down has what the coffee shop needs. This is the movement most likely to happen off the books unless the recorded path is the easy one.

Not built: a same-transaction exchangeTwo outlets swapping stock — milk one way, beans the other — as a single two-legged document was proposed and not built. Today that is two separate asks, one each way; nothing enforces that the second half actually happens, which is a real gap this note is naming rather than hiding.
07

Journey: production morning

Half past five, and the day's puffs and sandwiches do not exist yet. This is the flow that turns raw materials into something with a price.

Step 6's own ticket, and its codePushing finished goods out to an outlet (or dispatching a whole production order) raises a ticket exactly like step 3's, with its own six-digit code known only to the receiving outlet while it is open, and the same labelled supervisor override for an absent collector, open to the store keeper and the kitchen in-charge. (Landed in the Phase 6 fix wave — before it, every kitchen handover ran silently as the override, with no code and no choice.)
08

Journey: buying what ran out

Two roles, an external party, and the longest chain in the system. Its distinguishing feature is that it starts with a decision the store keeper makes, not a computation nobody can override.

09

Journey: closing the day — not built

The stage most systems skip, and the one flow chart v1 imagined this way. None of it exists. There is no shift, no wastage entry, no cash-variance workflow, no bill cancellation or credit note, and no day lock — a session is the signed-in person, not a terminal bound to a drawer for a period that closes (spec §3, out of scope; docs/ua-spec.html §09 records the same decision by name). Read the rest of this walk as the shape a future release would take, not a description of the app.

Counter Operator21:30 · POS
Stops taking orders and opens shift close. The screen lists everything still on the counter, split by whether it survives the night. Class A traded goods → roll over  ·  Class B short-life → must be cleared
Counter Operator21:35 · Adjustments
Records wastage for unsold short-life stock with a reason code. Nine puffs at ₹17.79 go to the wastage account, not into tomorrow's opening stock. − 9 nos counter  →  + ₹160.11 wastage  ·  closing stock for that item is now zero
Counter Operator21:40 · POS
Declares cash in the drawer. The system compares it against the tender recorded across the shift and records the difference — a variance is explained, never absorbed. declared ₹18,420  ·  system ₹18,460  ·  variance −₹40 with a note
Outlet Manager21:45 · Approvals
Signs off the wastage and the cash variance. Above the auto-approve threshold both need a second pair of eyes, which is the whole point of having a supervisor on the floor.
System21:45
Locks the shift. From here a bill can no longer be cancelled — it can only be reversed by a credit note, which is a different document with its own approval. day locked against back-dated entry
Finance22:00 · Reports
Reconciles UPI and card settlement against the tender report, then runs the day reconciliation across every location. opening + receipts + production − issues − sales − consumption − wastage ± adjustments = closing
Why wastage is made deliberately visible rather than convenientIt would be easy to let unsold stock roll forward silently, and every counter would prefer it. But the daily wastage value per outlet is the single most useful signal you will get about whether the production plan matches real demand — a sandwich sell-through under 70% for four days running means the kitchen is making too many, and no other number tells you that.

11

Document lifecycles

Four documents carry the system. Each has a fixed set of states, and each state names who is holding it.

Stock request

Draft→Submitted→ Under review→Approved / Partial→ Ticket issued→Collected→ In transit→Received→Closed

Branches: Rejected at the decision, which issues no ticket at all. A trimmed approval's undelivered balance stays open rather than being silently dropped. As built, a reservation does not expire — there is no window and no timer; an uncollected ticket holds its stock until it is handed over or explicitly cancelled, which is the one door back.

Pick ticket

Issued→Collected — source debited→ In transit→Received — destination credited→Closed

Branches: Cancelled — a ticket nobody has collected yet can be withdrawn by the store keeper, the kitchen in-charge, or (since Phase 6) the counter that raised a shop-to-shop transfer, at the location it was issued from; the hold it placed is released and the request or production order behind it goes back to exactly where it stood before the ticket was raised, and a withdrawn transfer's shop-ask returns to the other shop's desk. Once a ticket has been handed over, cancelling it is no longer offered — the stock is already moving, and there is no scanned or expired state: it is handed over on the collector's own six-digit code, typed by the issuing desk, or the labelled supervisor override.

Every screen watching a document updates itselfThe request → approval → issue → handover → receipt chain above runs on the server, and every open screen holds one live connection to it. The outlet manager does not refresh the approval queue to see a request the counter just raised, and the counter does not refresh to see the store keeper's ticket — the screen already changed by the time either of them looks.

Production batch

Started→Made — ingredients off, units on, one transaction

A batch is refused whole, before anything moves, if the kitchen cannot cover the recipe at the quantity started — there is no partial make and no unexplained-variance gate; the batch row itself (started vs. made) is the only record of a shortfall, and nothing edits it after the fact.

The kitchen's board and its batches run on the serverAccepted → in kitchen → ready → dispatched is the server's own walk, one press at a time, and closing a batch is too: the recipe's ingredients and the finished units are booked in one transaction, so the two can never disagree. A batch that cannot cover its recipe is refused whole, naming the ingredient that is short rather than partially consuming it. And the units that reach the rack are the ones that actually came good — a batch started at 60 and yielding 58 books 58, not 60, and the difference is what the batch record keeps.

Purchase order

Draft→Ordered→ Partially received→Received

Branches: Cancelled, only from Draft or Ordered with nothing yet received — a partly received order cannot be cancelled, only closed short. Received with a short note when the balance will never arrive and the buyer closes it short instead of waiting. There is no vendor-invoice matching and no payment status in this build — “Received” is where the document's own life ends.

One rule spans all fourNothing is ever deleted and no stock move is ever edited — stock_moves refuses UPDATE and DELETE at the database level; a correction is a new, reversing move. That is what makes db:rebuild-balances reproduce every balance from the moves alone (docs/ua-spec.html UA-24), which is the actual reconciliation gate, not the day close §09 describes as unbuilt.
The buying chain runs on the serverThe store keeper raises a requisition against the central store; it is the buyer, not the store keeper, who decides it — approving it whole, trimming it line by line, or declining it. An approved line is then picked onto a draft purchase order, sent to the vendor, and received in one or more instalments against a delivery note. Every one of those steps is the server's own, and every open screen watching a requisition or an order updates itself the moment another browser moves it, the same as the request chain above. A claim comes back the way it went out: cutting a line, dropping it, cancelling the order, or closing it short with a balance still outstanding all hand the undelivered quantity straight back to the requisition it was picked from, so nothing that was asked for is ever quietly lost between the two documents. A delivery that arrives more than 2% over what was ordered is refused whole, not partially booked — the store keeper holds it for a purchase decision rather than accepting an over-delivery no one approved. Whatever quality check turns away at the door goes to a quarantine shelf that never sells and never issues; there is no screen or endpoint that moves stock back out of it.
12

Where it breaks

Every handoff in this document is a place where a person can decide the software is slower than a phone call. These are the six most likely, what happens next, and what the design does about it.

The shortcutWhat goes wrongWhat the design does
Stock handed over without a ticket Source shows stock it no longer has, destination sells what the system says it does not hold. Both counters' figures are wrong and neither knows. The ticket, not the approval, is what opens the store room. A supervisor override exists for out-of-hours — at the store or the kitchen only — but it skips the collector's six-digit code on a ticket that already exists, not the ticket itself, and it writes one line to the audit trail: "Handed over — supervisor override".
Receipt never confirmed Quantity sits in transit forever. It belongs to nobody, so nobody chases it. A stranded hold is found by querying open reservations against tickets not yet received (deploy/RUNBOOK.md §8) — not a dashboard alert today, a query. There is no expiry: it waits until someone looks, receives it, or cancels it.
Production entered after the fact Raw material consumption is guessed after the fact, so the unit cost behind every price is fiction. Finished goods cannot exist without a batch, and a batch consumes its ingredients and books its yield in the same transaction it is made in. Nothing can be issued to an outlet that the kitchen never recorded making.
Wastage rolled forward instead of written off — not built There is no shift close and no wastage entry to skip. Unsold short-life stock simply stays on the shelf until it is sold, switched off by hand, or corrected with a hand-written adjustment move.
FEFO proposal overridden habitually — not built There is no batch-wise stock and no FEFO proposal to override — each item is one flat balance per location. Nothing in this build picks a batch, so nothing can pick the wrong one.
One login shared across a counter — different problem now There is no PIN and no shift to share; a session is one signed-in person's employee id and password. The real risk this build carries instead is a password shared between two people, which the audit trail cannot see either — a reason to rotate a shared credential the moment it is suspected, not a gap the software closes.
The one that matters mostOf the five, the ticket is the load-bearing one. If stock moves between floors without a document, nothing downstream in this design works — not the availability calculation, not the reconciliation, not the requirement run. Everything else in this document assumes that every physical movement has a ticket and a code behind it, not a phone call. That assumption is worth defending in training more than any feature is worth building.
