One Store, Many Counters

Eateasy already knows how to take an order and split a bill across outlets. It has no idea what a bottle of milk is. This document works out the inventory half — one item master behind every counter in the building, stock that requests itself, and a ticket that is the only way anything physically leaves a store.

01

How the world does it

Healthcare food inventory is a solved problem with a settled shape. Two vendor families dominate it, and the multi-outlet restaurant world independently arrived at the same loop. Worth knowing before we design, because it tells us which parts are non-negotiable.

Hospital foodservice software abroad splits into clinical nutrition suites and catering operations suites, and the leaders do both. Computrition runs inventory across catering, cafés, dining halls, hospital lounges and cook-chill finished goods in one solution, with floor stock tracked per cost centre. CBORD couples menu and recipe planning to nutritional analysis, production and inventory, and generates orders from planned menus, par levels or templates with receiving that updates stock and price automatically. In the UK, Civica Catering Management — the system formerly called Saffron, in use at 275+ hospital sites — puts purchasing, stock control, menu engineering, supplier management, recipes, allergen management, food safety and cleaning schedules in a single tablet-based product.

Separately, the multi-unit restaurant industry built the same machine for commissary kitchens. The loop described by MarketMan and Supy is exactly the one Royal Care needs: outlet sales draw down outlet stock, low outlet stock raises an indent to the central kitchen, the kitchen's production run draws down raw materials, and low raw materials draft a supplier order. Crucially, stock requests are actioned only after review and approval by the central kitchen manager — approval is a designed step, not an afterthought.

Five patterns worth copying

One item master, many stock locations

Cafés, catering, dining halls and ward floor stock are locations inside one inventory, each mapped to a cost centre — not separate systems that reconcile monthly.

Computrition inStock

Recipes are infrastructure

With a recipe on every made item, the system knows what usage should have been. Actual-versus-theoretical variance becomes the single number that exposes theft, over-portioning and waste.

CrunchTime · Supy

Requisition before movement

Nothing moves between locations on a phone call. A request is raised, reviewed, edited if needed, and approved before a single unit leaves the shelf.

Supy commissary

Compliance lives in the same system

Allergens, temperature logs and cleaning schedules sit beside stock rather than in a separate folder, because the inspector asks for all of it together.

Civica · FSSAI Schedule 4

Location-aware retail

Hospital café POS products handle many small counters under one roof with location-based inventory, inter-store transfers and per-location menu and price scheduling.

ARBA hospital cafeteria

What none of them assume

Every one of these treats retail counters, patient meals and ward stock as one inventory. Splitting them is the mistake to avoid, not a simplification to reach for.

Synthesis
The Indian compliance layer they don't coverA hospital canteen needs a State FSSAI licence regardless of turnover, and inspection runs against Schedule 4 GMP/GHP: food-handler health records, FoSTaC training certificates, temperature monitoring and pest control. Civica bundles the equivalent UK obligations into the catering system; we should do the same here rather than leaving a paper file, because the data the inspector wants — batch, supplier, temperature, who handled it — is already passing through this system.
02

What Eateasy gives us

I read the repo. It is a well-built food-court ordering and billing platform — and the half we need is almost entirely absent, which is good news, because nothing has to be undone.

Eateasy models a mall: malls → food_courts → kitchens → menu_items, with QR-coded tables, a single cart that fans out into orders → sub_orders (one per kitchen) → order_items, and a Razorpay money trail through payments, refunds, ledger_entries, settlements and payouts. Roles are customer_role, kitchen_role, admin_role and super_admin_role, enforced in TanStack Router beforeLoad guards. Stack is Hono on Bun, Drizzle, PostgreSQL, Better-Auth, Zod, React 19 and shadcn.

That hierarchy maps onto the hospital without a fight: mall becomes the hospital, food court becomes a floor or zone, kitchen becomes an outlet — the restaurant, each floor's shop, the central kitchen itself. Seat-based QR ordering becomes bedside and table ordering. The multi-outlet single cart is genuinely useful here: a visitor buys a coffee from the third-floor shop and a sandwich from the restaurant on one bill, and each outlet sees only its own line.

The building

One central store, one central kitchen, a restaurant, a shop on several floors, and pantries that consume without selling. Every one of them is a stock location in a single ledger.

Location types decide behaviour

One table drives most of the system's validation. A location's type is what makes it impossible to sell from a quarantine bin or receive a vendor delivery straight into a ward pantry.

The shops brew coffee and tea, so they are not pure retail — they hold beverage raw materials and run mini-recipes. That single fact is why every outlet needs a stock location, not just the kitchen.

04

Request, approve, ticket

The mechanism you described, worked out properly. One document type covers every internal movement, and the ticket is the only thing that opens a store room door.

A single stock request handles replenishment from the central store, a production request to the kitchen, a lateral pull from another outlet, and a return or exchange going the other way. What differs is the source and therefore who approves; the lifecycle is identical, which keeps one queue, one audit trail and one report instead of four.

The design decision everything else hangs from: approval and movement are separate events. Approving a request does not move a single unit. It authorises a quantity and reserves it. Stock leaves the source only when someone scans the ticket at the counter, and arrives only when someone scans it again at the destination. Between those two scans the quantity sits in transit, owned by neither location — which is precisely how you find out that six litres of milk left the store and four arrived.

Rules that make it hold

Approve line by line, not all or nothing

Asked for 20 litres, 12 available. The approver grants 12, and the remaining 8 stays open as a back-order against the next goods receipt rather than disappearing and being re-requested tomorrow.

Approval reserves, with a clock

Approved quantity is held at the source so two outlets cannot both be promised the same last carton. Uncollected reservations expire after a set window and the stock returns to available — otherwise approved-but-forgotten requests slowly freeze the store.

The ticket is the authority

Number plus QR, naming source, destination, item, approved quantity, batch and collector. Presented at the store, scanned, and only then does anything move. No ticket, no goods, whatever anyone says on the phone.

Both ends scan

The receiving outlet confirms what actually arrived. Difference between picked and received is a discrepancy with an owner and a reason code, not a rounding error absorbed by the store.

Inventory can push, too

The same document runs in reverse: the store originates a transfer against par level, or recalls near-expiry stock from a slow outlet to a busy one. The outlet acknowledges rather than requests. One document, a direction flag.

Emergency path, still recorded

An out-of-hours handover lets the store or the kitchen wave a collector through on a supervisor override instead of the usual code — the ticket already exists, only the code is skipped. The escape hatch exists because it will be used; making it a logged path is better than pretending it will not happen. It writes one line to the audit trail naming exactly what ran: Handed over — supervisor override.

// what the approver is actually looking at
available to promise = on hand − reserved − allocated to production − quarantined
// and the number that decides whether to approve at all
days of cover = available to promise ÷ average daily consumption at that location
Every open screen hears it happenThe approver's queue, the store's issue desk and the counter's own request list all watch one persistent connection per browser rather than polling. A write commits, Postgres NOTIFYs every server replica, and each one forwards the notice down every stream it is holding; the browser that receives it re-reads only the slice that changed. A request approved in one window appears in another's queue with no reload and no delay worth noticing.
05

Peer transfer and exchange

Sourcing from another counter in the same building is faster than sourcing from the store, and it is the movement most likely to happen off the books unless the system makes it easy.

The third-floor coffee shop runs out of milk at 4 pm. The store is closed, the restaurant on the first floor has twelve litres. Today that is a phone call and an unrecorded walk down two floors, and the stock ledger is wrong until someone notices at month end. So the system has to make the recorded path the easy path.

A lateral request differs from a store request in one respect that matters: the giving outlet approves it. Central store consent is not the control, because the store no longer owns the stock. The outlet manager who is being asked to give up twelve litres is the one who decides, and the central store is notified so the movement stays visible in a single ledger rather than becoming a private arrangement between two counters.

Why exchange is one document and not twoAn exchange posted as two independent transfers leaves a window where one outlet has given away stock and not yet received anything, and if the second half is forgotten the books stay wrong in a way nobody is chasing. Posting both legs in a single transaction means it either happens completely or not at all — and it gives the near-expiry redistribution the shops will actually use a clean audit line: 4 L milk → Floor 1, 2 kg coffee → Floor 3, one document, both managers' approval on it.
06

Items that switch themselves off

Eateasy already has the on/off switch. Wiring it to stock is a small change with a large effect: a customer can no longer order something the counter cannot make.

menu_items.is_available is a hand-flipped boolean today. It becomes a computed state with a mode, so that most items look after themselves and staff intervene only for the real exceptions.

ModeAvailable whenUsed for
STOCK_LINKED tradedOutlet on-hand for that item is above zeroBottled juice, packaged snacks. Switches off at the last unit sold, back on when a transfer is received
RECIPE_LINKED madeEvery recipe ingredient has enough at that outlet's location for at least one portionCoffee, tea, sandwiches. The UI names the binding ingredient: "Cappuccino off — milk 0.0 L at Floor 3"
SCHEDULEInside the item's time windowBreakfast items, the existing available_from / available_until columns
MANUALWhatever a human last setMachine broken, chef's call. Reason required, auto-clears at shift close so nothing stays wrongly off for a week

The switch-off is also a request

An item going dark is a demand signal, so the system should not merely alert. Crossing the low threshold drafts a stock request addressed to the right source — central store for traded goods, central kitchen for made items, and it offers a nearby outlet holding surplus as a lateral alternative. The outlet person confirms or edits and submits. This is the loop closing: the counter never types a request from scratch, and the inventory desk sees demand appear before anyone has run out.

One guard railAuto-off must respect an unfulfilled order. If three cappuccinos are already paid for and in the queue, the ingredients behind them are committed at the moment the order is accepted, not at the moment it is served — otherwise the counter cheerfully sells the last of the milk twice in the same minute.
07

Floor-wise pricing

Different prices on different floors is the one requirement Eateasy's schema actively fights, because price is a single column on the item.

The tempting shortcut is to make each floor's shop its own outlet with its own menu items — which works, right up to the point where "Cappuccino" exists six times, has six recipes to keep in step, and consolidated reporting has to guess that they are the same drink. It also makes a recipe change a six-place edit.

The right shape is one catalog item, many price list entries. A price list is scoped and effective-dated; an outlet points at one. Resolution runs outlet override first, then the floor or zone list, then the hospital base price. Because entries carry valid_from and valid_to, a bill raised today can still be explained in a year.

// price resolution at the counter
price = outlet_override(item) ?? floor_price_list(item) ?? base_price(item)
final = price × customer_level_modifier // visitor 1.00 · staff 0.75 · patient attender 1.00
// and one hard ceiling that no floor and no level may cross
if item.type = TRADED then final ≤ batch.printed_mrp

Two axes, deliberately kept apart. Location explains why a coffee costs more in the private-ward lounge than at the ground-floor kiosk. Customer type explains why a nurse pays less than a visitor at the same counter. Fold them into one number and the subsidy becomes invisible — and someone will eventually ask what the staff canteen actually costs the hospital, which is a question only a separately modelled subsidy can answer.

Carried over from the UA documentFor traded goods the printed MRP on the received batch is an absolute ceiling, and it is a batch property, not an item property. If the third floor's price list says ₹22 and the counter is deducting a ₹20 batch, the bill must be ₹20. No floor, no customer level and no approval overrides that.
08

Feeding procurement

Procurement should never receive a hand-typed wish list. What inventory sends is a computed requirement, with the arithmetic attached.

The requirement is assembled from four inputs the system already holds: items below reorder point, open outlet requests that cannot currently be fulfilled, the coming period's production plan exploded through recipes into raw materials, and consumption velocity over a trailing window. Netted against stock on hand and quantity already on order, that produces a suggested purchase list grouped by vendor.

// suggested quantity for one raw material
demand = unfulfilled requests + (production plan × recipe qty) + (avg daily use × lead time days)
suggested = demand + safety stock − on hand − on order
// rounded up to the vendor's pack size, never below the minimum order quantity

The store keeper reviews and edits that list, then submits it as a purchase requisition — the formal handover from inventory to procurement. Procurement takes it from there: rate contract lookup, quotation comparison for off-contract items, vendor selection, PO by the approval slabs in the companion document, and goods receipt back into the central store.

The piece that is usually missed: when that receipt posts, the outlet requests that were waiting on it wake up. A back-ordered line links to the requisition, the requisition to the PO, the PO to the GRN. The outlet gets told that its request for six tins of oil can now be fulfilled, and the store keeper sees a ready-to-approve queue rather than a fresh round of identical requests. Without that chain, unfulfilled requests die silently and every shortage is requested three times.

The three habits the system has to enforce

Cycle counting, not an annual stocktake

High-value and fast-moving items counted weekly, everything else on a rotation, so the book and the shelf never drift far enough to be unexplainable. The system schedules the count and refuses to let the counter see the expected figure until after it is entered.

Par review on real consumption

Reorder points set once at go-live become wrong within a month. A quarterly prompt shows actual consumption against the configured par and proposes new numbers.

Expiry sweep before it becomes waste

Near-expiry stock at a slow counter is a redistribution opportunity for about a week and a write-off after that. A daily list, with the recall or exchange one click away.

The one architectural rulestock_moves is append-only and is the only source of truth. Every other table — receipt, transfer, production, sale, wastage, adjustment — writes moves and never edits a balance. stock_balances is a maintained cache that can be rebuilt from the moves at any time. Nothing is ever deleted; a mistake is corrected by a reversing move that carries the id of what it reverses. This is what makes the day-reconciliation test in the companion document provable rather than merely hoped for.
The production module, as builtTwo writes answer the kitchen's board and its production runs: one that walks a production order across its stages one press at a time, and one that closes a batch. Closing a batch is one transaction over the ledger — the recipe's ingredients leave and the finished units arrive in the same commit, so there is no instant at which the books show one without the other, and a batch that cannot cover its recipe is refused whole rather than partially consumed.

Where the sale meets the stock

The join between Eateasy and everything above is one hook on the order-status transition. When an order_item reaches its terminal served state, depletion runs: a traded item deducts one unit of itself at the outlet's location; a made item deducts its recipe lines. It must be idempotent, since order status can be written more than once, and reversible, since Eateasy already supports per-item kitchen cancellation with cancelled_at. A depleted_at stamp on the order item is the guard: set it in the same transaction as the moves, and skip if it is already set.

Build order

Four phases, each one usable on its own. The sequencing is deliberate: nothing later can be built honestly without the ledger from phase one.

PHASE 1

The ledger

Item master, UOM, locations, append-only stock moves, goods receipt, opening stock. Depletion hooked to Eateasy's order flow, and stock-linked availability. At the end of this phase, selling something changes a number that is actually true.

PHASE 2

Movement

Stock requests, approval routing, reservations, pick tickets with QR scan at both ends, lateral transfer, exchange, return and recall. The whole of §04 and §05.

PHASE 3

Making and losing

Recipes, production batches, yield and consumption variance, recipe-linked availability, wastage, cycle counts, adjustments. Theoretical versus actual becomes measurable.

PHASE 4

Buying and pricing

Requirement run, purchase requisition, vendors and rate contracts, PO, three-way match, back-order reawakening. Price lists, customer levels, MRP ceiling. Compliance logs.

Why availability is in phase 1 and not laterIt is the smallest visible win in the whole build — the counter stops selling what it does not have on day one — and it forces the depletion hook to be correct early, while the system is small enough to debug. Deferring it means discovering the hard parts of depletion during phase 3, with three more subsystems already leaning on it.
12

Your decisions

Eight this document turns on, on top of the ten in the UA specification.

Are any outlets leased to third parties?

If a coffee brand runs the lobby counter, Eateasy's settlement and payout machinery is exactly right and stays. If the hospital owns every outlet, payouts become internal cost-centre transfers and a chunk of the existing money code changes purpose. This is the biggest single fork in the design.

Do the shops actually produce, or only resell?

You said coffee, tea and snacks. Brewing on site means each shop holds raw materials, runs mini-recipes and needs recipe-linked availability — a materially bigger build than pure retail counters.

Does the restaurant cook, or plate what the central kitchen sends?

Decides whether production exists in one place or several, and whether the restaurant needs its own raw stock and yield tracking.

Who physically carries stock between floors?

Porters, outlet staff, a service lift with time windows? The pick ticket can name a courier and timestamp both ends, but only if there is a person to name. This decides whether in-transit is a real accountable state or a formality.

How many price levels, and who funds the difference?

Visitor, staff, doctor, patient attender. If staff pay a subsidised rate, the gap has to land in a cost centre someone owns, or the outlet's margin silently absorbs it.

Can an outlet ever refuse a lateral request?

Our design says yes — the giving manager approves. If hospital policy is that the store's word is final and outlets must comply, the routing changes and outlet managers lose a control.

Is Eateasy the codebase we extend, or the reference we rebuild from?

Extending is faster and inherits working auth, ordering and payments. Rebuilding gives a clean hospital-shaped schema without mall vocabulary. Our recommendation is to extend — the ordering and money code is the expensive part and it already works.

How far does patient food reach into this?

Ward pantries are in the topology above as consuming locations. Whether patient diet planning itself lives here or in the separate HIMS module is still Q1 of the UA document and still open.
